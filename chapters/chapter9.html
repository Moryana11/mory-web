Chapter IX. – Days from the Ashes

The Passage of the Key — James's Point of View Little James, barely ten years old, pressed himself against the cold stone wall in one of the forgotten alleyways of the city of Herba. Rain dripped from his hair and soaked his shirt, which was dirty and far too large for him. The city remained noisy even in the darkness. The cries of street vendors blended with the clatter of horses' hooves and the occasional shout of someone who had lost money, food, or simply their illusions. James held a piece of soggy bread in his hands, something he had found behind one of the market stalls. Sitting down on the ground, he hungrily bit into his first meal in two days. The cold stung his ears, but he tried not to make a sound. He knew that in Herba, the weak were shown no mercy. He watched the passersby, paying attention to every movement and every glance. He learned quickly—who was friendly, who was dangerous, and who might offer a little food to a starving child. Even then, the caution and determination to survive that would later guide him down a path he could never have imagined were already taking root within him. Suddenly, he heard a rustling sound nearby. James froze. A few steps away, a black-haired boy about three years younger than him was crouching in the shadows. Half hidden behind a pile of discarded crates, he stared with dark eyes filled with hunger and fear. Without saying a word, James held out his hand and offered him half of his bread. The boy hesitated for a moment, then nodded and sat down beside him on the ground. That day, they became brothers. James looked after him, teaching him how to survive, where to hide, and how to find food quickly. The smaller boy was slower, clumsy, and easily distracted, yet James remained patient with him and never once got angry. One evening, after the rain had left the streets slick and nearly deserted, they spotted a drunken man lying on the ground in one of the narrower alleyways. The younger boy paused for a moment, his eyes lighting up. He saw an opportunity to take something they could trade for a proper meal. Creeping toward the man, he reached for the coin pouch tucked into his pocket. “Wait!” James whispered, his voice quiet but urgent. “It’s not safe! Stay behind me. I’ll go first!” The younger boy frowned at him for a moment, but the determination in his eyes did not fade. He crouched beside the drunken man and quickly slipped a hand into his coat. As he tried to run back to James and proudly show off his prize, he tripped and fell. The sound echoed through the silent alley. The man stirred and opened his eyes. His furious gaze immediately locked onto the boy, and he grabbed him with a strong hand. The child struggled desperately to break free, but the drunken man was far stronger. James froze. In that instant, he knew he had to act. “No!” the older boy shouted and threw himself at the man. The drunkard refused to release the younger boy. One hand remained tightly clamped around the child's arm while the other shoved James so hard that he crashed onto the wet ground. Then the man began kicking him. Each blow landed hard and painfully. James writhed, trying to protect his face and get back on his feet, but every attempt was met with another kick. Still, he refused to back down. Fear for the younger boy, whom the man still held captive, burned in his eyes alongside a fierce determination to get him to safety. Meanwhile, the younger boy struggled desperately to help James. He twisted in the man's grip, kicking and pushing, but to no avail. When he saw James remain motionless on the ground after another blow, he leaned forward and sank his teeth into the man's hand with all his strength. “Ow! You little bastard!” the drunkard roared. But he did not let go. Instead, his grip tightened even further. The younger boy cried out in pain. Tears welled in his eyes, yet he did not stop fighting. He tried once more to pull free, but it was useless. James lay on the rain-soaked ground, every part of his body aching as he struggled to catch his breath. When he saw the tears in the boy's eyes, he forced himself to lift his head and rasped, “Leave him alone.” The man looked at him. For a moment, he paused and studied him thoughtfully. Something flickered in his eyes, as if an idea had just occurred to him. Then he smirked. “You've got guts, brat.” He kicked James twice more before grabbing him by the throat. Effortlessly lifting him into the air, he dragged both boys to a run-down tavern where the air reeked of damp wood, sweat, and cheap alcohol. With a boy in each hand, he pushed his way toward a table where a hooded man sat in the shadows. Most of his face was hidden beneath the hood, but his eyes glimmered in the faint lamplight. The drunkard leaned against the table and nodded toward the boys. “I heard,” he said, his voice thick with alcohol, “that you're looking for young boys.” The hooded man slowly raised his eyes and gave a brief nod. The drunkard leaned across the table and smirked. “I’ve got two for sale. They’re young, but capable. Feed them a little, and you can do whatever you want with them.” The hooded man lifted his head and calmly asked, “How much do you want for them?” The drunkard thought for a moment before grinning. “Five gold coins.” The hooded man slowly shook his head. “They’re not worth that much,” he said calmly, his voice cold and devoid of emotion. “I’ll offer eight copper coins. Five for the older one and three for the younger.” The drunkard’s face immediately darkened with anger. “What? The older one fought me in the street!” The hooded man merely smirked faintly. His gaze remained fixed on the two boys. After a moment, he tossed eight copper coins onto the table. “Take the money—or keep them,” he said calmly, in a voice that knew no mercy. “Fine. The brats are yours now.” The drunkard quickly snatched up the coins and headed for the bar to buy another bottle without looking back. James and the younger boy were helpless, but James remembered every detail—every blow, every glance, every sound. The hooded man leaned closer, his eyes still hidden beneath the shadow of his hood. “Listen carefully,” he began calmly, his voice deep and uncompromising. “The world you are about to enter is nothing like the streets you have wandered until now. Here, only those who learn to fight survive. Those who are not afraid of pain. Those who know their own worth.” James felt his heart pounding, but the man continued in the same cold tone. “Do not expect mercy. Do not expect anyone to offer you a helping hand. You will be trained. You will be tested. The strong will survive, and the weak will die.” The boys stood by the table, tense and silent. The younger child pressed himself against James while his fear mingled with a growing sense of dread. The hooded man smiled briefly, though there was no warmth in it. “But don’t be afraid. If you endure and learn what must be learned, you will become something worthy of respect. Just remember—survival is a game where pain is the rule and weakness is a luxury you cannot afford.” Then he fell silent, gave a slight nod, and stepped back into the shadows. Yet his presence still hung heavily over the boys. James knew that their lives had changed forever—and that the road ahead would be harsh, merciless, and filled with darkness. The hooded man rose to his feet, took James by the shoulder, and led the boys through the streets of Herba, which seemed even colder and more hostile in the darkness. James held the younger boy’s hand tightly, feeling him tremble, and he knew he had to be strong now—not only for himself, but for him as well. The journey was long and dark. Narrow alleyways gave way to forgotten courtyards and crumbling gates until they finally arrived at a towering stone gate secured with heavy iron bars. The man led them through the city’s main gate, across a stone bridge, and farther along rocky paths winding between trees and cliffs. After an hour of walking, a distant estate finally came into view. When they arrived, they were not admitted through the main entrance but through a small back door hidden among the trees. They did not go up into the manor. Instead, they were led down a narrow stone staircase that descended beneath it. There, a mage and his assistant were already waiting for them. The mage was short, plump, and balding. He wore a friendly expression, but his eyes were sharp and cold—the eyes of a viper that knew no mercy. His assistant, quiet and vigilant, watched the boys sternly through a pair of round spectacles. “Welcome,” the mage said cheerfully, though his voice carried an unspoken threat. “I hope you're prepared.” He slowly approached them and began examining them as though they were not people at all, but objects whose value and potential needed to be carefully assessed. His hands tested the firmness of their muscles, the flexibility of their joints, and the tension in their bodies. “Hm... they're thin,” he muttered as his fingers ran along the older boy’s arm. “They don’t look like they’ll survive what’s coming. We may need to buy new ones if this fails.” The hooded man stood nearby, silent and motionless, observing the mage’s every movement. “The older one,” he finally said calmly, “fought a drunkard in the street. We’ll see how much he can endure.” The mage narrowed his eyes and slowly looked James over before turning his gaze to the younger boy. His expression remained cold and analytical, without the slightest trace of sympathy. “Good,” he murmured quietly. “We shall see.” That very night, they were taken to a laboratory beneath the manor—a cold chamber filled with metal tables, cabinets holding strange instruments, and the faint greenish glow of lamps. The walls smelled of chemicals and damp stone. The first months of experimentation were brutal. The day began before sunrise and ended long after sunset. The mage tested them in countless ways—sometimes through physical trials, other times through psychological ones. Every failure, every weakness, was meticulously recorded on parchment. Dark rituals were performed on them and the other children they encountered there every single day. Before and after the rituals, the boys were forced to drink various potions. Some clouded their minds, others burned through their veins, and still others made their hearts race so violently that they could hear the pounding of their own heartbeat echoing inside their heads. James was often restrained in cold iron shackles, though he barely noticed their chill. The only thing he felt was endless pain. The younger boy suffered just as much. However, his body was smaller, more fragile, and weaker, making every ordeal far harder for him to endure. As the days passed, the boys grew more and more exhausted, their bodies covered in bruises and scars. Then came the day when the mage’s rituals took an even darker turn. The boys watched as more and more children failed the trials. Small lifeless bodies collapsed to the floor, their faces frozen in the suffering they had endured before death. Strange changes appeared on their skin—claws replacing fingernails, bark-like growths spreading across their flesh, as though the ritual had tried to transform their bodies but had failed. James learned to focus on only one thing: breathing. When others succumbed to panic, he repeated the same words to himself: Inhale... exhale... inhale. The rhythm of his heartbeat became his refuge. As long as he could breathe, he was alive. And as long as he was alive, there was still hope. “You see,” the mage muttered one day as he watched the younger boy collapse beneath the weight of another trial, “weakness is not forgiven here.” There was only cold curiosity in his eyes—the detached interest of a scientist observing an insect trapped inside a jar. Months passed. James could feel the younger boy changing as well. His childhood helplessness gradually disappeared, leaving behind only determination and resilience. Pain had become a teacher, and fear a tool for survival. The worst of the trials were over, but peace never came. Only change. Instead of shackles, they were given weapons. The daggers were dull and worn, their chipped edges tearing more than cutting. Every strike hurt their hands, and every fall left behind a new scar. From a distance, the mage observed their training and recorded everything on his parchments—who attacked without hesitation, who defended themselves, and who hesitated. “A hired killer is not a knight,” the hooded man often said while the boys struggled to catch their breath. “Honor means nothing to him. He does not seek glory or recognition. He cares only about the result—a dead target and his own survival.” James soon learned to recognize the rhythm of combat just as he had once learned to focus on his breathing. He watched. He studied. He paid attention to footwork, the angle of a strike, the weaknesses of an opponent. At first, the older boys beat him regularly. But in time, he became the one others avoided. He was neither the biggest nor the strongest. But he was fast. And above all, he was calm. The younger boy no longer trembled when holding a dagger. On the contrary, his fingers gripped it with confidence, as though it were an extension of his own hand. All that remained in his eyes was a raw, untamed fury that flared whenever he smelled blood. He was James’s complete opposite. Where James observed, he acted. There was something wild inside him, something that refused to submit to discipline. While James learned to kill out of necessity, the boy was already beginning to find a twisted satisfaction in it. And the hooded man saw it. Instead of suppressing it, he smiled. Because that was exactly what he wanted them to become: Two daggers. The training was merciless. In the middle of the night, they would be awakened by orders to kill shadows conjured by the mage—illusions of people, animals, and demons. Every movement had to be lethal. Every strike had to be precise. Those who hesitated were beaten or denied food. Those who rebelled disappeared. No one ever asked where they had gone. After some time, the boys became figures dressed in black cloaks and black masks, moving silently like specters. The hooded man taught them that killing was not enough—they had to remain unseen, leave no trace, and know how to wait. “Killing,” he would say as he walked among them with his hands clasped behind his back, “is not an art. The art is making sure no one ever learns it happened.” And in the silent underground halls, where the screams of dying children had once echoed, there was now only the sound of weapons striking wood and the muted breathing of those who had learned how to survive. James was becoming one of the best. Not because he wanted to. But because he no longer knew how to be anything else. Years passed. The boys became young men. They moved with a grace that even seasoned swordsmen might have envied—silent, precise, and cold. One day, the hooded man summoned them. He stood beside a stone table in a chamber so quiet that they could hear droplets of water falling from the ceiling. Resting on the stone were four black daggers. Their blades gleamed as though they absorbed light rather than reflected it. James and his brother stood opposite him. They asked no questions. Both of them knew the time had come. The hooded man slowly ran his fingers along one of the blades, almost as if he were caressing it. “You have trained for a long time,” he said in a muffled voice distorted by his mask. “You have spent years learning how to survive. Now we will see whether you are capable of more than that.” He extended his hand. Two daggers were given to each of them. The cold metal felt heavier in their palms than they had expected. “Today, you will kill for the first time. Not for me,” he continued, “but for yourselves. For the past that made you what you are.” James did not move. He merely tightened his grip on the hilts. His brother, on the other hand, smirked. The familiar predatory smile appeared at the corner of his mouth. “Who?” he asked. The hooded man was silent for a moment. “The man who sold you,” he replied simply. The air seemed to grow heavier. Even without another word, they understood. That day, they would not be killing because they had been ordered to. They would be killing because of the thing that had turned them into monsters. The night they set out was just as cold as the night they had been sold. Rain hammered against the rooftops, and the streets lay deserted. Their target lived in a small village beyond the city walls, in a crumbling cottage surrounded by the stench of cheap liquor. He was a broken man—once brutal, now little more than a shadow of his former self. James’s brother smirked when he spotted him through a cracked window. “So this is what we survived for?” he whispered. “Pathetic.” James said nothing. His hand did not even twitch. He simply watched as his brother crouched low and silently slipped inside. The sound of a footstep. A muffled gasp. And then—silence. James entered a moment later. The air inside smelled of blood and alcohol. The man lay sprawled across the floor while James’s brother stood over him, raising a dagger for another strike. There was more than hatred in his eyes. There was a fury that had slept for years and had finally awakened—raw, uncontrollable, and consuming. It mingled with contempt and a deep, visceral disgust. His handsome face was twisted with rage, his teeth clenched, his breathing ragged. Every breath felt like a scream of suppressed anger. And James... James simply stood there and watched. The hatred burning in his brother’s eyes was reflected in his own—not with the same intensity, but as a shadow of it. Cold. Quiet. Devoid of passion. “That’s enough,” James said softly. His brother turned toward him. “Enough? I don’t know that word.” The dagger came down again. And again. And again. Of the man who had once sold them, nothing remained but a heap of flesh and broken bones. There was no struggle in it. No redemption. Only violence returning to the place from which it had come. James watched. He did not try to stop him. He simply stood there, breathing—in, out, in, out—and thought that perhaps this moment was not an ending. Perhaps it was a beginning. Before they left the cottage, both of them put on their masks. Neither spoke. There was nothing left to say. The reason they had come had been fulfilled.
